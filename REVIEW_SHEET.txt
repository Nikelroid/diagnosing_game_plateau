[C1] The atlas measures support bits, and that is not entropy.

  Definition in the paper: support bits = log2 of the number of histories consistent with the
  acting player's information state. Reported as the UNWEIGHTED mean over distinct information
  states in the tree, pooled across seats. Deliberately not reach-weighted.
  The paper says this is an upper bound on posterior entropy and says nothing about the value of
  the hidden state.
  Coverage: 88 configurations, 79 OpenSpiel and 9 RLCard.
  Provenance: 45 closed form, 30 not measured, 11 exact, 2 resampled.
  Gin Rummy anchor: 30.1 bits, cross-checked across two engines.

[C3] The measuring instruments lied three times, once in our own apparatus.

  Failure 1, saturation: the resampling estimator returns exactly log2(80) = 6.32 for three games
  because the sample budget is 80. It is a right-censored lower bound, not an estimate.
  Failure 2, degeneracy: every Gin Rummy variant resamples to 0.00 bits with zero censoring,
  because the engine resampler returns the same world repeatedly.
  Failure 3, ours: in liars_dice(numdice=2,dice_sides=3) the PLACEBO arm scored 0.67 above its own
  exact ceiling, which no policy can do. The per-deal placebo draw is nearly unique with two dice,
  so the plane became a fingerprint of the deal and leaked what it was built to withhold. The game
  is rejected from every table and figure by an automated rule.

[C5] The plateau is not relieved by this information. That is narrower than learning-bound.

  The paper USED to say "this agent is learning-bound" and "effort belongs in optimisation, reward

[C6] A plateau splits into worth (the game) and capture (the learner).

  Worth = oracle ceiling minus baseline ceiling. Both ceilings are exact: best response to the
  opponent policy, and a memoised expectimax with the state revealed. No seed noise in either.
  Capture = what the learner banks, divided by worth.
  Worth is invariant to a 64-fold change in training budget (25k to 1.6M episodes), constant to
  three decimals in both games swept. Capture moves with budget: 56% to 79% in Leduc.
  Coverage: 11 games, 88 runs, 8 seeds each,
  1.00 to
  3.67 bits.

[C8] Capture exceeds 100% in seven games, and the reason is a known bias, not noise.

  7 of 11 games report capture above 100%, which is impossible if worth is the right
  denominator. The ceilings are exact, so the error is in the denominator's assumption: that the
  learner sits equally far below its ceiling in both arms.
  It does not. Revealing the state supplies information AND makes the task easier to learn.
  In all 7 games with capture > 100%, the baseline arm sits further below its own ceiling.
  In all 4 games with capture < 100% (the poker variants), the oracle arm does.
  Rank correlation between capture and the gap difference: exactly 1.000 over all 11 games,
  AND 1.000 within each of the two game families taken alone.
  Per game the effect is 0.8 to 2.3 times the seed spread, so no single game rules out noise.
  The paper deliberately does NOT quote a p-value: the games are related configurations.

[C9] What the paper does not do.

  One game and one expert in the case study. The 11 decomposed games are all small enough to solve
  exactly, which is the property that makes them unlike Gin Rummy.
  Capture is biased wherever the arms sit unequally below their ceilings, which is 7 of 11. The
  paper reports the sign and has no estimator that corrects it.
  No Gin Rummy training-budget sweep. No second frozen opponent. No structured oracle features.
  The architecture probe varies inductive bias and compute together. Neither it nor the bucket
  sweep tests capacity LOST during training, which is a live rival explanation for any plateau.
  Closed forms count hands and ignore public information; sized on one game only (Leduc, 2.01 exact
  against 2.30 closed form).

[C10] Positioning against prior work.

  Long et al. 2010 measured leaf correlation, bias and disambiguation factor and showed they predict
  when determinized search works. That is the precedent for the atlas. This paper had cited them
  only as a search method until an external round caught it.
  Markowitz et al. 2018 size information sets in reconnaissance blind chess against poker and Go.
  Schofield and Thielscher 2019 sample information sets and value information-gathering moves.
  Rebstock et al. 2019 do inference in trick-taking card games.
  The paper's stated novelty is now the JOINING: a provenance-aware measurement of what a domain
  hides, used to read a specific agent's plateau, with a placebo that prices the intervention.

[C11] Where this paper should go next. (Direction, not a blocker.)

  This section is NOT about the submission. Answer it as if the paper is already sent.
  Assume the deadline passed and everything above shipped as it stands.

  What the authors think they have: a workflow that measures what a domain hides, then reads a
  specific agent's plateau against it, with a placebo that prices the intervention itself.

  Where they think it could go, in no order and with no commitment:
    a. The blind spot. 30 of 88 configurations carry no number by any method,
       and 13 of those are board and fog-of-war games. Those are the games people
       call the frontier of imperfect information, and nobody can currently size them.
    b. The estimator. Support bits are an upper bound on posterior entropy. Measuring the entropy
       itself, under a stated policy, would say how far apart the two really are.
    c. The bias. Capture is biased wherever the two arms sit unequally below their ceilings, which
       is 7 of 11 games. There is no estimator here that corrects it, only one that
       reports its sign.
    d. Structured privileged features instead of a raw hand plane, to separate "the information is
       not worth much" from "this learner cannot use it in that form".
    e. Scale. Every exactly-decomposed game is small enough to solve, which is the property that
       makes it unlike the case study.

  The question: which of these is the next PAPER, and which are footnotes in this one? Name one
  you would drop entirely. If the real next step is not on the list, say what it is.

[A1] APPENDIX A. Reproduction

  Every table and figure is generated from files in data/ by a script in scripts/. Nothing in this paper is typed by hand. The atlas comes from scripts/atlas\_tables.py, the probe tables from scripts/probe\_tables.py, and the appendix tables from scripts/make\_appendix.py. Regenerating them from a clean checkout reproduces the paper.

  Ceilings are exact, not estimated. The baseline ceiling is the best response to the opponent policy, computed with OpenSpiel's BestResponsePolicy. The oracle ceiling is a memoised expectimax over the same game with the hidden state revealed. Both are closed-form values of the game, so they carry no seed noise and no confidence interval.

  Probe runs use eight seeds per game unless the table says otherwise. The Gin Rummy arms use twelve. Tabular runs use data/configs/; neural runs use a fixed-width tensor observation and policy gradient, matching the encoder the Gin Rummy learner uses.

[A2] APPENDIX B. The atlas in full

  Table[tab:app-corrections] lists the entries whose closed form we had to repair. The naive count treats every physical card as distinguishable, which is right for a 52-card deck and wrong for a deck with identical copies. Two red sevens are the same holding whichever card you were dealt, so a hand is a multiset and not a sequence. Counting them as distinct inflates the estimate. For Mahjong it inflates it by 67 bits, and for UNO it puts the game above a ceiling it cannot reach. The repair changes one claim in the main text: before it, two of 23 two-player card games hid more than Gin Rummy, and after it, one does.

[A3] APPENDIX C. What the probes measure

  The placebo arm is the part of the design most easily got wrong, so it is worth stating exactly. The placebo plane has the same shape and the same sparsity as the oracle plane. It holds ten cards this player cannot see, drawn independently of the opponent's actual hand, and it is resampled once per deal rather than once per decision. Resampling per decision would not leak, because a learner could average that noise away. But it would give the placebo arm a different input distribution than the oracle arm sees. One draw per deal keeps the two arms matched.

  An early version of the placebo drew from a pool of the wrong size. The plane was therefore not matched in cardinality to the oracle plane, and the arm priced something other than the widening. We fixed the pool and re-ran every affected game. The numbers reported here are from the re-run.

  We also verified that the oracle plane is what we say it is. PettingZoo's opponents\_hand\_visible flag does not supply the opponent's hand. It exposes an ``unknown cards'' plane, which holds 41 cards on a fresh deal against the opponent's 10, so a learner reading it cannot recover the hand. We built our own plane and checked it against engine ground truth at 1,200 decision points. Each curriculum opponent's input is trimmed to its own width, so the extra plane reaches the learner and nobody else.

[A4] APPENDIX D. The decomposition across eleven games

  The per-game numbers are in Table[tab:axis] in the main text. This section records how they were produced and what each column can carry.

  Worth is the oracle ceiling minus the baseline ceiling. Both are exact values of the game, so worth has no seed noise and no interval; the eight seeds per game affect the banked column alone. Banked is what the learner gains from the hidden state, measured as the oracle arm minus the baseline arm. Capture is banked over worth. Widening is the placebo arm minus the baseline arm.

  The eleven games are not eleven independent domains. Seven are Liar's dice at different die sizes and three are Leduc variants, so the spread of bits is wider than the spread of game structure. That is a deliberate choice: holding the game fixed and moving one parameter is what lets worth be compared across rows at all. It also means the seven-to-four split in Section[app:mechanism] tracks two game families, not seven independent confirmations, and we do not read it as such.

[A5] APPENDIX E. Worth does not move with the training budget

  If worth were a property of the learner, it would drift as the learner trained longer. It does not. [A6] APPENDIX F. What a capacity-bound plateau looks like

  The capacity probe answers a different question: not what the game hides, but whether the learner can tell enough situations apart. We fold information states into a fixed number of buckets and sweep that number. A learner that is capacity bound improves as the buckets increase and stops when it runs out of them.

  Leduc Hold'em is the clean case. Exploitability falls from 5.58 at two buckets to 1.55 with exact states, and the curve is monotone over the last three points. That is what being capacity bound looks like. Kuhn poker and Liar's dice are flat across the whole sweep, because both are small enough that two buckets already separate what matters. Dark hex on a two by two board is solved, so every arm reads zero. It is in the table as a control: the instrument should return zero when there is genuinely nothing to find, and it does.

[A7] APPENDIX G. The rejected game

  Every arm is graded against a ceiling that is a closed-form property of the game, so an arm scoring above its own ceiling is impossible rather than merely surprising. We check it automatically, and one game of twelve fails.

  In liars\_dice(numdice=2,dice\_sides=3) the placebo arm returns 0.738 against a ceiling of 0.062, a violation of 0.68, consistent across all eight seeds. The placebo plane is drawn once per deal so that its input distribution matches the oracle plane's. With two dice the space it is drawn from is large enough that the draw is close to unique per deal, which turns the plane into an identifier: the learner keys on it, splits states that should share a policy, and recovers the hidden information indirectly. The recorded state count shows the split, at 4046 visited states against the baseline's 3046.

  The one-die games are safe for the reason the two-dice game is not: their placebo alphabet is three to six values, far too coarse to fingerprint a deal. The fix is to match the placebo's cardinality to the hidden state rather than to its shape, which is a rerun we did not have time for. We exclude the game from every table rather than report it, and the rejection rule is in scripts/make\_appendix.py so no future run can enter a table unannounced.

[A8] APPENDIX H. Capture above one hundred percent

  Section[app:axis] reports capture above 100 percent in seven games. The body argues that this is a property of the denominator and not a measurement error. Table[tab:app-gaps] is the evidence, per game.

  For each arm we measure how far the trained learner sits below that arm's own exact ceiling. The difference between the two gaps is what capture's denominator silently assumes to be zero. It is not zero in any of the eleven games. It is negative in the four poker variants, where the oracle arm is the harder of the two to learn. It is positive in the seven dice variants, where the oracle arm is the easier. The sign matches the direction of capture's bias in every row. The rows are sorted by capture, so the flip falls exactly where capture crosses 100 percent.

  The ordering carries more than the sign does. Capture rises monotonically down the table and the gap difference rises with it, from -0.358 to +0.077. The rank correlation between the two is exactly 1.000 over all eleven games. We quote the ordering rather than a p-value: the games are related configurations and not independent draws, so any test would report a confidence the design does not support.

  That ordering is what answers the obvious objection to the sign count. Eleven games are not eleven independent trials. Seven are Liar's dice at different die sizes and four are Leduc and Kuhn variants. A sign that agrees eleven times may only be agreeing twice. The rank correlation is 1.000 within each family taken alone, over the seven dice variants and over the four poker variants separately. Inside a family the games differ by one parameter, and the ordering still holds, which a two-family coincidence does not explain.

  Per game the effect is smaller than that ordering suggests. In the dice variants the gap difference is 0.8 to 2.3 times the seed-to-seed spread of the banked column. No single game rules out noise on its own. The evidence is the ordering across games, not the margin within one. None of it gives the size of the correction. Knowing that capture reads high does not say by how much, and we have no estimator that recovers the unbiased value. Reporting the sign and the ordering is as far as this instrument goes.

  One reading we can rule out is a bug in the oracle ceiling. If the oracle ceiling were too low, capture would exceed 100 percent everywhere, and it does not. The four poker variants run the same ceiling code and land below 100, and the ordering runs through both families continuously.

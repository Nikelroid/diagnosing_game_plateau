\begin{abstract}
A win rate is only as informative as what it was measured against, and two things are almost never
measured: how much the game actually hides, and what an agent's plateau is made of. We supply both.
First, an atlas: across 88 games from two engines we compute the bits of hidden information at a
typical decision. Two-player card games saturate early, with a median of 3.1 bits and only one of 23
hiding more than Gin Rummy's 30.1; everything clearly above sits at a larger table, where extra
bits come from extra hands rather than a deeper duel. The atlas also catches its own instrument
lying in two directions: the resampling estimator returns a saturated constant for three games and
a confident \emph{zero} for a game with 30.1 bits. And it exposes a blind spot: of 19 board games,
the only six we can measure are the perfect-information controls, so every fog-of-war game in the
atlas is unmeasured. Second, three probes that turn a plateau against a fixed expert into a
diagnosis. On a Gin Rummy case study they agree: capacity is not the limit (every encoder lands in
21.2 to 30.0 percent), the value of hidden information is bounded but confounded, and an
oracle-observation learner handed the opponent's hand outright reaches \ORACLEOBSMEAN{} percent
against a \textbf{27.04} baseline over \ORACLEOBSN{} seeds, a difference of $-0.25$ points whose 95
percent interval, $[-1.78, +1.28]$, contains zero. Measuring the domain and
diagnosing the agent are one workflow, and we release the code and all measurements.
\end{abstract}

\section{Two measurements nobody makes}

Agents in imperfect-information games are graded against references that cannot bear the weight.
Random play yields numbers near 99 percent that barely move as the agent improves. Self-play yields
numbers near 50 percent by construction. A pool of past checkpoints moves as the agent moves, so
progress against it is not progress in any absolute sense. Fixing the reference is the obvious
repair, and it is not enough, because two questions survive it.

\textbf{How much does this game hide?} Games are chosen by reputation. Poker is hard, Hanabi is
cooperative, Stratego is big. The quantity that supposedly unites them is rarely computed, so
comparative claims about difficulty rest on tradition.

\textbf{What is the plateau made of?} The plateau we diagnose here is the one reported by
\citet{goldstandard}, whose agents stall well short of their fixed expert. An agent that stops
improving is stuck either because it
cannot see enough (information-bound) or because it can and fails to exploit it (learning-bound).
These call for opposite work, and one win rate cannot separate them.

We answer the first by measurement and the second by three probes, and the case study shows why
they belong in the same paper: without the atlas, a plateau invites the excuse that the game hides
too much; without the probes, the atlas is a table nobody acts on.

\section{Part I: an atlas of what is hidden}

Hidden information at a decision is $\log_2$ of the number of histories consistent with the acting
player's information state. Zero is perfect information; each further bit doubles the worlds the
player must consider. We compute it three ways and always report which.

\textbf{Exact} enumeration walks the tree and groups histories by information state. This is ground
truth, and it is affordable on 11 of our 88 rows. \textbf{Closed form} counts the ways the hidden
hands could have been dealt: $\log_2\binom{u}{k}$ for one hidden hand out of $u$ unseen cards, and
the multinomial for a full table, since it matters which opponent holds which cards. It is exact
arithmetic but counts hands only, ignoring what public cards have ruled out, so it overestimates
mildly; Leduc sizes that gap at 2.01 exact against 2.30 closed form. \textbf{Resampling} draws
worlds consistent with the information state and counts the distinct ones, censored at the sample
count.

The catalog spans 79 games from OpenSpiel \citep{openspiel}, the only engine here exposing
information-state strings and world resampling, and 9 from RLCard \citep{rlcard} for games OpenSpiel
does not ship, Mahjong and UNO above all. Gin Rummy is deliberately measured by both so the closed
form can be cross-checked across libraries; it agrees at 30.1 bits each. PettingZoo
\citep{pettingzoo} supplies the environment API for the case study.

\paragraph{Two-player card games saturate.} Their median is 3.1 bits, and of 23 only one hides more than standard Gin
Rummy's 30.1: bridge uncontested bidding, at 32.9, and that is a three-turn auction fragment
rather than a whole game.
A 52-card deck split between two hands cannot conceal much more. A difficulty ladder assembled from
distinct two-player card games therefore bunches at the top rather than spanning a range.

\paragraph{More hidden information means more players.} The three and four-handed family has a
median of 31.1 bits and reaches 109.6 for Mahjong, with Hearts, Spades and contract bridge at 56.2.
Those bits are bought with extra hands and arrive attached to coalitions and signalling, which is a
different problem rather than a deeper version of the same one. If a difficulty \emph{axis} is what
is wanted, it has to come from a dial inside one game: our eight Gin Rummy configurations hold the
rules fixed and move only the deck and hand size, sweeping 5.1 to 30.1 bits.

\paragraph{The blind spot.} Thirteen fog-of-war games, dark chess and reconnaissance blind chess
among them, carry no number by any method: too large to enumerate, no deal to count, and no
resampling implemented. These are the games most often called the frontier of imperfect information,
and they are exactly the ones nobody can currently size.

\section{When the instrument lies}

Resampling is what a practitioner reaches for when a game is too large to enumerate. In our data it
fails silently in both directions, and returns a plausible number each time.

\textbf{Saturation.} Hearts, Euchre and contract bridge each returned exactly 6.32 bits, which is
$\log_2 80$, the sample count, with 100 percent of sampled points censored. The estimator had run
out of samples, not of worlds; the closed form puts Hearts at 56.2, nearly nine times higher.

\textbf{Degeneracy.} Worse, because it looks like a clean result: every Gin Rummy configuration
returns \emph{0.00} bits with zero censoring, since the engine's resampler hands back the same world
on every draw. A survey trusting that column alone would file a 30-bit game beside Connect Four.

Neither failure is visible without a second estimator to disagree with. That is the practical case
for reporting provenance in every row, which our released table does.

\section{Part II: three probes for a plateau}

The atlas says what a domain can hide; it cannot say whether a given agent is limited by that. Our case
study reuses the setting of a recent study of lightweight Gin Rummy agents \citep{goldstandard},
which grades against a fixed, deterministic, cheap rule-based expert that never trains:
it grades 600 games in about two minutes, and the graded agent answers in 0.3\,ms per move.

\paragraph{The subset argument, which needs no experiment.} Our expert reads two of the
learner's four observation planes, so its observation is a strict subset. It scores about 50
percent against itself, so parity is well defined, and any policy reaching parity is a function of
information the learner already receives. This transfers to any domain where the reference is a
restriction of the learner.

\paragraph{Probe 1: capacity.} Train the identical recipe with different encoders, as in the
architecture sweep of \citet{goldstandard}. A plain MLP
averages 25.5 percent over four seeds and a self-attention encoder 25.7, with all eight seeds inside
21.2 to 30.0. Attention buys variance, not mean, at roughly twelve times the wall-clock cost, so the
network is not the binding constraint.

\paragraph{Probe 2: the value of information, as an upper bound.} Run a determinized search
\citep{pimc,ismcts} graded fairly, then as an oracle handed the true hidden cards, following
\citet{goldstandard}. Fair search
reaches 10.3 percent at 10 rollouts and 25.6 at 120; the oracle variant reaches 40.7 and 85.3 on
identical budgets. The gap bounds what the hidden information is worth without pricing it, because oracle
access also removes strategy fusion \citep{strategyfusion,rebel}.

\paragraph{Probe 3: the oracle-observation learner.} Retrain the identical recipe with the hidden
state appended to the observation and change nothing else, putting the question to the learner
rather than to a search agent. Handed the opponent's hand outright, it reaches $26.79$
percent across twelve seeds against a $27.04$ baseline, a difference of $-0.25$ points with a 95
percent interval of $[-1.78, +1.28]$ (Figure~\ref{fig:probes}). An earlier four-seed version of
this probe had an interval of $[-4.5, +2.7]$, which could not exclude an eleven percent relative
gain, so the null is only as good as the seeds behind it.

A third arm decides what that null means. Widening an observation is not free: the network gains
inputs it must learn to ignore, and any cost of that is subtracted from whatever the real plane is
worth. We therefore run a \emph{placebo} plane of the same shape and sparsity, holding ten cards
this player cannot see, drawn independently of the opponent's actual hand. It scores $27.02$
percent, $-0.03$ points from baseline. There is no widening cost for a benefit to cancel.

Two details decide whether the probe measures what it claims. PettingZoo's
\texttt{opponents\_hand\_visible} flag does \emph{not} supply the opponent's hand: it exposes an
``unknown cards'' plane holding 41 cards on a fresh deal against the opponent's 10. We verified our
plane against engine ground truth at 1{,}200 decision points. And we trim each curriculum
opponent's input to its own width, so the extra plane reaches the learner alone.

\paragraph{Does the probe work at all?} A null is worth nothing without evidence the
instrument can fire. We ran the identical probe on every OpenSpiel game small enough that
counterfactual regret minimisation supplies a true optimum, under two learners: a tabular one and
a neural one reading a fixed-width tensor, as the Gin Rummy learner does. It detects the hidden
state everywhere it is present, by margins of $0.22$ to $1.07$ in expected return, and the placebo
lands on the baseline every time. On Gin Rummy the same construction reads zero
(Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity}).

\section{Part III: splitting a plateau in two}

Where a true optimum is computable, the probe does more than return a verdict. It decomposes the
plateau. The best return achievable with the hidden state, minus the best achievable without it, is
what the information is \emph{worth}. Both terms are exact: the first is a memoised expectimax over
the revealed game, the second a best response to the opponent policy. Neither carries seed noise.
What fraction of that the learner actually banks we call \emph{capture}.

The two behave as a game property and a learner property should. Worth does not move. We swept training
budgets over a sixty-four-fold range, from 25{,}000 to 1{,}600{,}000 episodes. In both games worth
is constant to three decimals at every budget (Appendix~\ref{app:budget}). Capture moves with the budget
instead, climbing from $56$ to $79$ percent in Leduc Hold'em. A plateau therefore separates into a
quantity set by the game and a quantity set by the agent, which is the distinction a win rate
cannot make.

Table~\ref{tab:axis} runs the decomposition across eleven games. The widening column is what makes
the Gin Rummy null mean anything. Across all 88 runs it averages $+0.018$, so a wider observation
costs the learner nothing measurable. A null in the oracle arm is therefore not a real gain
cancelling a real cost, which is the reading a placebo exists to rule out.

\paragraph{Capture can exceed one hundred percent, and the reason is not noise.} Seven of the
eleven games report capture above $100$ percent. At face value that is impossible, and the ceilings
are exact, so the error is not in them. It is in what capture assumes. Capture divides by a
difference between two ceilings, which is the right denominator only if the learner sits equally
far below its ceiling in both arms. It does not. Handing over the hidden state supplies information
\emph{and} makes the task easier to learn, because the oracle arm no longer maintains a belief over
what it cannot see.

The two effects are separable in our data, and the pattern is exact. In all seven games where
capture exceeds $100$ percent, the baseline arm sits further below its own ceiling than the oracle
arm does. In all four where capture falls below $100$ percent, which are the four poker variants,
the oracle arm sits further below instead. The sign of that gap difference predicts the direction
of capture's bias in eleven games out of eleven (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}). The two quantities
are perfectly rank correlated, and remain so within each of the two game families taken alone
(Appendix~\ref{app:mechanism}).

\begin{figure}[t]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\linewidth]{fig_mechanism.pdf}
  \caption{Why capture can exceed one hundred percent. Each game is plotted by how far each arm
  sits below its own ceiling. The oracle does not only supply information, it also changes how hard
  the task is to learn, and the two effects share a denominator.}
  \label{fig:mechanism}

That makes capture a biased statistic with a known sign, not an unreliable one. Where the oracle
arm learns more easily, capture reads high; where it learns less easily, capture reads low. Read as
a bound it still says something: the poker learners bank at least the $73$ to $78$ percent shown.
Read as a point estimate it says less than it appears to, and we do not use it as one. The Gin
Rummy null never divides by worth, so this bound does not reach it.

\section{Reading both halves together}

The atlas places Gin Rummy at 30.1 bits, within 4 of the two-player ceiling, so the agent is graded
in a genuinely information-rich game rather than a thin one. The probes then say it is not held back
by that information: capacity is not the limit, the subset argument puts parity inside the learner's
own information, and handing over the hidden state changes nothing measurable. The effort belongs in
optimisation, reward and curriculum, not in belief modelling.

\paragraph{When no strong reference exists.} Most domains have no hand-codable expert. In descending
order of what they buy: the strongest available heuristic, frozen, reported with its saturation
point; a frozen pool with a fixed Elo anchor; a snapshotted checkpoint promoted to reference and
never updated; or, best where possible, a reference deliberately built on a subset of the learner's
observation, which makes parity provably reachable by construction. Equilibrium baselines
\citep{cfr,nfsp,deepcfr} give an absolute reference where the game is small enough to solve.

\paragraph{Limitations.} The case study is one game and one expert, and the search bound is
confounded with strategy fusion. The decomposition covers eleven games, but all of them are small
enough to solve exactly, which is the same property that makes them unlike Gin Rummy. Capture is
biased wherever the two arms sit unequally far below their ceilings, which is seven of those
eleven; we report the sign rather than correcting it, because we do not yet have an estimator that
does. The capacity probe covers the encoders we could train at scale. The closed forms count hands
and ignore public information. Bits are a property of the game and not of the difficulty of
learning it: we claim the two are routinely conflated, not that they are unrelated.

\section{Conclusion}

Choosing a domain is a measurement problem and reading a plateau is a diagnostic one, and both are
usually settled by tradition. Eighty-eight games measured, three probes, eleven games decomposed:
this agent is learning-bound, in a game that genuinely hides a great deal. The decomposition also
prices its own instrument, and says where capture can and cannot be read as a number.

\section{What the probes measure}
\label{app:apparatus}

The placebo arm is the part of the design most easily got wrong, so it is worth stating exactly.
The placebo plane has the same shape and the same sparsity as the oracle plane. It holds ten cards
this player cannot see, drawn independently of the opponent's actual hand, and it is resampled once
per deal rather than once per decision. Resampling per decision would not leak, because a learner could average that noise away. But it
would give the placebo arm a different input distribution than the oracle arm sees. One draw per deal keeps the two arms matched.

An early version of the placebo drew from a pool of the wrong size. The plane was therefore not
matched in cardinality to the oracle plane, and the arm priced something other than the widening.
We fixed the pool and re-ran every affected game. The numbers reported here are from the re-run.

We also verified that the oracle plane is what we say it is. PettingZoo's
\texttt{opponents\_hand\_visible} flag does not supply the opponent's hand. It exposes an
``unknown cards'' plane, which holds 41 cards on a fresh deal against the opponent's 10, so a
learner reading it cannot recover the hand. We built our own plane and checked it against engine
ground truth at 1,200 decision points. Each curriculum opponent's input is trimmed to its own
width, so the extra plane reaches the learner and nobody else.

\section{The decomposition across eleven games}
\label{app:axis}

The per-game numbers are in Table~\ref{tab:axis} in the main text. This section records how they
were produced and what each column can carry.

Worth is the oracle ceiling minus the baseline ceiling. Both are exact values of the game, so worth
has no seed noise and no interval; the eight seeds per game affect the banked column alone. Banked
is what the learner gains from the hidden state, measured as the oracle arm minus the baseline arm.
Capture is banked over worth. Widening is the placebo arm minus the baseline arm.

The eleven games are not eleven independent domains. Seven are Liar's dice at different die sizes
and three are Leduc variants, so the spread of bits is wider than the spread of game structure.
That is a deliberate choice: holding the game fixed and moving one parameter is what lets worth be
compared across rows at all. It also means the seven-to-four split in Section~\ref{app:mechanism}
tracks two game families, not seven independent confirmations, and we do not read it as such.

\section{Worth does not move with the training budget}
\label{app:budget}

If worth were a property of the learner, it would drift as the learner trained longer. It does not.
Table~\ref{tab:app-budget} runs the decomposition at 25,000 to 1,600,000 episodes, a
sixty-four-fold range. Worth is constant to three decimals at every budget in both games, which is
what a game property should do. Capture climbs instead, from 56 to 79 percent in Leduc Hold'em.

Kuhn poker is the useful contrast. Its capture does not climb monotonically: it dips to 83 percent
at 400,000 episodes and returns to 97 at 1,600,000. The game is small enough that the learner is already near its ceiling at the smallest budget. What
the middle budgets show is seed noise, not a trend. We report it rather than smoothing it away.

\section{What a capacity-bound plateau looks like}
\label{app:capacity}

The capacity probe answers a different question: not what the game hides, but whether the learner
can tell enough situations apart. We fold information states into a fixed number of buckets and
sweep that number. A learner that is capacity bound improves as the buckets increase and stops when
it runs out of them.

Leduc Hold'em is the clean case. Exploitability falls from 5.58 at two buckets to 1.55 with exact
states, and the curve is monotone over the last three points. That is what being capacity bound
looks like. Kuhn poker and Liar's dice are flat across the whole sweep, because both are small
enough that two buckets already separate what matters. Dark hex on a two by two board is solved, so every arm
reads zero. It is in the table as a control: the instrument should return zero when there is
genuinely nothing to find, and it does.

\section{Capture above one hundred percent}
\label{app:mechanism}

Section~\ref{app:axis} reports capture above $100$ percent in seven games. The body argues that
this is a property of the denominator and not a measurement error. Table~\ref{tab:app-gaps} is the
evidence, per game.

For each arm we measure how far the trained learner sits below that arm's own exact ceiling. The
difference between the two gaps is what capture's denominator silently assumes to be zero. It is
not zero in any of the eleven games. It is negative in the four poker variants, where the oracle arm is
the harder of the two to learn. It is positive in the seven dice variants, where the oracle arm is
the easier. The sign matches the direction of capture's bias in every
row. The rows are sorted by capture, so the flip falls exactly where capture crosses $100$ percent.

The ordering carries more than the sign does. Capture rises monotonically down the table and the
gap difference rises with it, from $-0.358$ to $+0.077$. The rank correlation between the two is
exactly $1.000$ over all eleven games, with a linear correlation of $0.981$ at $p = 9 \times
10^{-8}$.

That ordering is what answers the obvious objection to the sign count. Eleven games are not eleven independent trials.
Seven are Liar's dice at different die sizes and four are Leduc and Kuhn variants. A sign that
agrees eleven times may only be agreeing twice. The rank correlation is
$1.000$ \emph{within each family taken alone}, over the seven dice variants and over the four poker
variants separately. Inside a family the games differ by one parameter, and the ordering still
holds, which a two-family coincidence does not explain.

Per game the effect is smaller than that ordering suggests. In the dice variants the gap difference
is $0.8$ to $2.3$ times the seed-to-seed spread of the banked column. No single game rules out
noise on its own. The evidence is the ordering across games, not the margin within one.
None of it gives the size of the correction. Knowing that capture reads high does not say by how
much, and we have no estimator that recovers the unbiased value. Reporting the sign and the
ordering is as far as this instrument goes.

One reading we can rule out is a bug in the oracle ceiling. If the oracle ceiling were too low,
capture would exceed $100$ percent everywhere, and it does not. The four poker variants run the
same ceiling code and land below $100$, and the ordering runs through both families continuously.

\section{The Gin Rummy arms in full}
\label{app:gin}

Table~\ref{tab:app-gin} is the full version of Figure~\ref{fig:probes}, with the spread reported
rather than drawn. All three arms overlap. The oracle arm is 0.25 points below baseline and the
placebo arm is 0.03 points below, and both intervals contain zero and contain each other's means.

Twelve seeds per arm is what it took to say this. An earlier four-seed version of the same probe
had an interval of $[-4.5, +2.7]$ on the oracle arm, which could not exclude an eleven percent
relative gain. A null is only as good as the seeds behind it, and four was not enough.

[C1] The atlas measures support bits, and that is not entropy.

  Definition in the paper: support bits = log2 of the number of histories consistent with the
  acting player's information state. Reported as the UNWEIGHTED mean over distinct information
  states in the tree, pooled across seats. Deliberately not reach-weighted.
  The paper says this is an upper bound on posterior entropy and says nothing about the value of
  the hidden state.
  Coverage: 88 configurations, 79 OpenSpiel and 9 RLCard.
  Provenance: 45 closed form, 30 not measured, 11 exact, 2 resampled.
  Gin Rummy anchor: 30.1 bits, cross-checked across two engines.

[C6] A plateau splits into worth (game and opponent) and capture (learner).

  Worth = oracle ceiling minus baseline ceiling. Both ceilings are exact, and BOTH are computed
  against the same fixed opponent policy; the only difference is whether the acting learner sees the
  hidden state. Worth is therefore exact for that evaluation setting, not for the game in the
  abstract. No seed noise in either ceiling.
  Capture = what the learner banks, divided by worth.
  Worth is invariant to a 64-fold change in training budget (25k to 1.6M episodes), constant to
  three decimals in both games swept. Capture moves with budget: 56% to 79% in Leduc.
  Coverage: 11 games, 88 runs, 8 seeds each,
  1.00 to
  3.67 bits.

[C8] Capture exceeds 100% in seven games, and the algebra says why.

  CHANGED SINCE ROUND ONE. Two reviewers found that the previous version of this claim treated an
  algebraic identity as empirical evidence. They were right. The claim is now the identity.

  7 of 11 games report capture above 100%, which looks impossible. Write g_b and g_o for
  how far each arm sits below its own exact ceiling. Then, from the definitions alone:

      banked = worth + g_b - g_o

  so capture exceeds 1 if and only if g_b > g_o. Always. Our runs satisfy the identity with a maximum
  residual of 1.1e-16, which is floating point.

  The paper previously reported that the sign agreed "in eleven games out of eleven" with a rank
  correlation of 1.000, as though that confirmed a mechanism. It confirms nothing: the agreement is
  guaranteed by the definitions. Both the count and the correlation are removed.

  What is still empirical is where each family sits. The dice arms are 0.000 to 0.007 below their
  oracle ceilings, because with the die revealed the game is nearly solved. The poker arms are 0.377
  to 0.394 below theirs, because the oracle ceiling is much higher. That split is the finding.
  Per game the gap difference is 0.8 to 2.3 times the seed spread, so no single game settles it.

[C9] What the paper does not do.

  One game and one expert in the case study. The 11 decomposed games are all small enough to solve
  exactly, which is the property that makes them unlike Gin Rummy.
  Capture is biased wherever the arms sit unequally below their ceilings, which is 7 of 11. The
  paper reports the sign and has no estimator that corrects it.
  No Gin Rummy training-budget sweep. No second frozen opponent. No structured oracle features.
  The architecture probe varies inductive bias and compute together. Neither it nor the bucket
  sweep tests capacity LOST during training, which is a live rival explanation for any plateau.
  Closed forms count hands and ignore public information; sized on one game only (Leduc, 2.01 exact
  against 2.30 closed form).

[C10] Positioning against prior work.

  Long et al. 2010 measured leaf correlation, bias and disambiguation factor and showed they predict
  when determinized search works. That is the precedent for the atlas. This paper had cited them
  only as a search method until an external round caught it.
  Markowitz et al. 2018 size information sets in reconnaissance blind chess against poker and Go.
  Schofield and Thielscher 2019 sample information sets and value information-gathering moves.
  Rebstock et al. 2019 do inference in trick-taking card games.
  The paper's stated novelty is now the JOINING: a provenance-aware measurement of what a domain
  hides, used to read a specific agent's plateau, with a placebo that prices the intervention.

[C11] Where this paper should go next. (Direction, not a blocker.)

  This section is NOT about the submission. Answer it as if the paper is already sent.
  Assume the deadline passed and everything above shipped as it stands.

  What the authors think they have: a workflow that measures what a domain hides, then reads a
  specific agent's plateau against it, with a placebo that prices the intervention itself.

  Where they think it could go, in no order and with no commitment:
    a. The blind spot. 30 of 88 configurations carry no number by any method,
       and 13 of those are board and fog-of-war games. Those are the games people
       call the frontier of imperfect information, and nobody can currently size them.
    b. The estimator. Support bits are an upper bound on posterior entropy. Measuring the entropy
       itself, under a stated policy, would say how far apart the two really are.
    c. The bias. Capture is biased wherever the two arms sit unequally below their ceilings, which
       is 7 of 11 games. There is no estimator here that corrects it, only one that
       reports its sign.
    d. Structured privileged features instead of a raw hand plane, to separate "the information is
       not worth much" from "this learner cannot use it in that form".
    e. Scale. Every exactly-decomposed game is small enough to solve, which is the property that
       makes it unlike the case study.

  The question: which of these is the next PAPER, and which are footnotes in this one? Name one
  you would drop entirely. If the real next step is not on the list, say what it is.

[A1] APPENDIX A. Reproduction

  Every table and figure is generated from files in data/ by a script in scripts/. Nothing in this paper is typed by hand. The atlas comes from scripts/atlas\_tables.py, the probe tables from scripts/probe\_tables.py, and the appendix tables from scripts/make\_appendix.py. Regenerating them from a clean checkout reproduces the paper.

  Ceilings are exact rather than estimated. The baseline ceiling is the best response to the opponent policy, computed with OpenSpiel's BestResponsePolicy. The oracle ceiling is a memoised expectimax over the same game with the hidden state revealed. Both are closed-form values of the game, so they carry no seed noise and no confidence interval.

  Probe runs use eight seeds per game unless the table says otherwise. The Gin Rummy arms use twelve. Tabular runs use data/configs/; neural runs use a fixed-width tensor observation and policy gradient, matching the encoder the Gin Rummy learner uses.

[A3] APPENDIX C. What the probes measure

  The placebo arm is the part of the design most easily got wrong, so it is worth stating exactly. The placebo plane has the same shape and the same sparsity as the oracle plane. It holds ten cards this player cannot see, drawn independently of the opponent's actual hand, and it is resampled once per deal rather than once per decision. Resampling per decision would not leak, because a learner could average that noise away. But it would give the placebo arm a different input distribution than the oracle arm sees. One draw per deal keeps the two arms matched.

  An early version of the placebo drew from a pool of the wrong size. The plane was therefore not matched in cardinality to the oracle plane, and the arm priced something other than the widening. We fixed the pool and re-ran every affected game, so the numbers reported here are from the re-run.

  We also verified that the oracle plane is what we say it is. PettingZoo's opponents\_hand\_visible flag does not supply the opponent's hand. It exposes an ``unknown cards'' plane, which holds 41 cards on a fresh deal against the opponent's 10, so a learner reading it cannot recover the hand. We built our own plane and checked it against engine ground truth at 1,200 decision points. Each curriculum opponent's input is trimmed to its own width, so the extra plane reaches the learner and nobody else.

[A4] APPENDIX D. The decomposition across eleven games

  The per-game numbers are in Table[tab:axis] in the main text. This section records how they were produced and what each column can carry.

  Worth is the oracle ceiling minus the baseline ceiling. Both are exact for the stated opponent policy, so worth alone carries no seed noise. The eight seeds per game reach banked and widening directly, and reach capture through banked. Banked is what the learner gains from the hidden state, measured as the oracle arm minus the baseline arm. Capture is banked over worth, and widening is the placebo arm minus the baseline arm.

  The eleven games are not eleven independent domains. Seven are Liar's dice at different die sizes and three are Leduc variants, so the spread of bits is wider than the spread of game structure. That is a deliberate choice: holding the game fixed and moving one parameter is what lets worth be compared across rows at all. It also means the seven-to-four split in Section[app:mechanism] tracks two game families, not seven independent confirmations, and we do not read it as such.

[A5] APPENDIX E. Worth does not move with the training budget

  If worth were a property of the learner, it would drift as the learner trained longer. It does not. Table[tab:app-budget] runs the decomposition at 25,000 to 1,600,000 episodes, a sixty-four-fold range. Worth is constant to three decimals at every budget in both games, which is what a game property should do. Capture moves instead, rising from 56 to 79 percent in Leduc Hold'em. In Kuhn poker it dips at the middle budgets before returning, so the pattern is a trend in Leduc and not a law.

  Kuhn poker is the useful contrast. Its capture does not climb monotonically: it dips to 83 percent at 400,000 episodes and returns to 97 at 1,600,000. The game is small enough that the learner is already near its ceiling at the smallest budget. What the middle budgets show is seed noise rather than a trend, and we report it rather than smoothing it away.

[A6] APPENDIX F. What a capacity-bound plateau looks like

  The capacity probe answers a different question: not what the game hides, but whether the learner can tell enough situations apart. We fold information states into a fixed number of buckets and sweep that number. A learner limited by state resolution improves as the buckets increase, then stops when it runs out of them.

  Leduc Hold'em is the clean case. Exploitability falls from 5.58 at two buckets to 1.55 with exact states, and the curve is monotone over the last three points. That is the signature we would expect from a state-resolution bottleneck, on a single sweep without replication. Kuhn poker and Liar's dice are flat across the whole sweep, because both are small enough that two buckets already separate what matters. Dark hex on a two by two board is solved, so every arm reads zero. It is in the table as a control: the instrument should return zero when there is genuinely nothing to find, and it does.

[A7] APPENDIX G. The rejected game

  Every arm is graded against a ceiling that is a closed-form property of the game. An arm scoring above its own ceiling is therefore impossible, not merely surprising. We check it automatically, and one game of twelve fails.

  In liars\_dice(numdice=2,dice\_sides=3) the placebo arm returns 0.738 against a ceiling of 0.062, a violation of 0.68, consistent across all eight seeds. The placebo plane is drawn once per deal so that its input distribution matches the oracle plane's. With two dice the space it is drawn from is large enough that each draw is close to unique. The plane then acts as an identifier. The learner keys on it, splits states that should share a policy, and recovers the hidden information indirectly. The recorded state count shows the split, at 4046 visited states against the baseline's 3046.

  The one-die games are safe for the reason the two-dice game is not: their placebo alphabet is three to six values, far too coarse to fingerprint a deal. The fix is to match the placebo's cardinality to the hidden state rather than to its shape, which is a rerun we did not have time for. We exclude the game from every table rather than report it, and the rejection rule is in scripts/make\_appendix.py so no future run can enter a table unannounced.

[A8] APPENDIX H. Capture above one hundred percent

  Section[app:axis] reports capture above 100 percent in seven games, and the body derives why. This section gives the derivation in full and the per-game numbers behind it.

  The identity. Let c_b and c_o be the exact baseline and oracle ceilings, and let r_b and r_o be what the trained arms actually score. Worth is c_o - c_b and banked is r_o - r_b. Each arm's gap below its own ceiling is g_b = c_b - r_b and g_o = c_o - r_o. Substituting gives % \[ g_b - g_o \;=\; (c_b - r_b) - (c_o - r_o) \;=\; (r_o - r_b) - (c_o - c_b) \;=\; banked - worth, \] % which rearranges to Equation~eq:identity. Our runs satisfy it with a maximum residual of 1.1 10^-16, which is floating point and nothing else.

  Two things follow from that. First, capture exceeds 1 if and only if g_b > g_o. Second, and this matters for how the result is read, that equivalence is guaranteed by the definitions. Counting how many games obey it would measure nothing. An earlier draft of this paper reported the count as though it were evidence, and it is not.

  What is empirical. The identity says the two quantities move together. It does not say which side of 100 percent any game lands on, or by how much. Those are facts about our runs, and Table[tab:app-gaps] reports them. The dice variants sit close to their oracle ceilings, with g_o between 0.000 and 0.007, because with the opponent's die revealed the game is nearly solved. The poker variants sit far from theirs, with g_o between 0.377 and 0.394, because the oracle ceiling is much higher and the learner does not reach it. The split into two families is the result; the sign agreement is arithmetic.

  What we can rule out. One reading is a bug in the oracle ceiling. If it were too low, capture would exceed 100 percent everywhere, and it does not. The four poker variants run the same ceiling code and land below. A second reading is that the effect is too small to trust. Per game the gap difference is 0.8 to 2.3 times the seed spread of the banked column, so no single game settles it on its own. We report the two-family pattern rather than a test, because eleven related configurations do not supply eleven independent trials.
